Introduction</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:ind w:firstLine="200"/><w:jc w:val="both"/><w:spacing w:after="120"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:sz w:val="19"/><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">Multilayer dielectric thin films are one of the oldest and still most widely used tools of optical engineering. Their optical response is produced by interference between the partial reflections at the layer interfaces, so the reflectance spectrum is controlled by only three families of design parameters: the number of layers, the materials (refractive indices) and the thickness of each layer [1,2]. Because the layers are thin compared with the wavelength, a modest change in thickness strongly modifies the phase accumulated by the wave in that layer, and therefore the position and depth of the interference fringes. Even for the simple four-layer stack considered here, the mapping from thickness to spectrum is strongly non-linear, and the frequency of the fringes grows with the total optical thickness.</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:ind w:firstLine="200"/><w:jc w:val="both"/><w:spacing w:after="120"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:sz w:val="19"/><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">The standard physical model of such a stack is the transfer matrix method (TMM) [1,3]. Each layer is represented by a 2x2 characteristic matrix that depends on the optical phase thickness; multiplying the matrices gives the input admittance of the multilayer, from which the amplitude reflection coefficient and the reflectance follow analytically. TMM is exact for the idealised model used here (plane wave, normal incidence, non-dispersive, lossless layers) and it is computationally cheap per design - microseconds to milliseconds. The difficulty appears when a very large number of designs has to be evaluated, for instance when 10,000 candidates must be ranked before a design is chosen: the cost grows linearly with the number of candidates and dominates the whole optimisation loop.</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:ind w:firstLine="200"/><w:jc w:val="both"/><w:spacing w:after="120"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:sz w:val="19"/><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">Machine-learning surrogate models offer a way out of this trade-off. A neural network trained on TMM data learns the mapping from design parameters to optical response directly from examples [4], and once trained it evaluates a design in a single forward pass that is orders of magnitude cheaper than a numerical simulation. Such surrogates are now routinely used in the inverse design of optical multilayer structures, either as vectorised regressors or as generative models [4-7]. The price is that a surrogate is only an approximation: it can mis-rank nearly equivalent candidates, and its accuracy depends on how much training data are available. The recommended reading for this assignment reviews this development from classical optimisation to deep learning [4].</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:ind w:firstLine="200"/><w:jc w:val="both"/><w:spacing w:after="120"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:sz w:val="19"/><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">This work addresses three questions for a deliberately minimal system - a four-layer Air/H/L/H/L/Glass stack with fixed refractive indices and only the four thicknesses as free parameters. (Q1) Can an MLP predict the reflectance spectrum of the stack accurately? (Q2) How does the prediction error change when the number of training samples grows? (Q3) Can the MLP act as a surrogate that helps to screen a large candidate library and find a design with high reflectance at a personal target wavelength? All three questions are answered with a single fixed dataset, a single fixed train/validation/test split and explicitly recorded random seeds, so that every number reported below can be reproduced. Figure 1 summarises the workflow: TMM generates the data, the MLP is trained on it, the surrogate ranks a new candidate library, and TMM verifies the finalists.</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:ind w:firstLine="0"/><w:jc w:val="center"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:noProof/><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:drawing><wp:inline distT="0" distB="0" distL="0" distR="0"><wp:extent cx="6316220" cy="2456307"/><wp:effectExtent l="0" t="0" r="0" b="0"/><wp:docPr id="1" name="Picture 1"/><wp:cNvGraphicFramePr><a:graphicFrameLocks xmlns:a="http://schemas.openxmlformats.org/drawingml/2006/main" noChangeAspect="1"/></wp:cNvGraphicFramePr><a:graphic xmlns:a="http://schemas.openxmlformats.org/drawingml/2006/main"><a:graphicData uri="http://schemas.openxmlformats.org/drawingml/2006/picture"><pic:pic xmlns:pic="http://schemas.openxmlformats.org/drawingml/2006/picture"><pic:nvPicPr><pic:cNvPr id="1" name="fig1_workflow.png"/><pic:cNvPicPr/></pic:nvPicPr><pic:blipFill><a:blip r:embed="rIdFig1"/><a:stretch><a:fillRect/></a:stretch></pic:blipFill><pic:spPr><a:xfrm><a:off x="0" y="0"/><a:ext cx="6316220" cy="2456307"/></a:xfrm><a:prstGeom prst="rect"><a:avLst/></a:prstGeom></pic:spPr></pic:pic></a:graphicData></a:graphic></wp:inline></w:drawing></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:ind w:firstLine="0"/><w:jc w:val="center"/><w:spacing w:before="60" w:after="180"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:b/><w:sz w:val="18"/><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">Figure 1. Materials and Methods</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:pStyle w:val="21"/><w:ind w:firstLine="0"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">2.1 Optical model and transfer matrix method</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:ind w:firstLine="200"/><w:jc w:val="both"/><w:spacing w:after="120"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:sz w:val="19"/><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">The object of the study is the four-layer stack Air / H / L / H / L / Glass, illuminated at normal incidence by a plane wave. The layers are non-absorbing and non-dispersive with refractive indices n_H = 2.30 (layers 1 and 3) and n_L = 1.45 (layers 2 and 4); the substrate is glass with n_s = 1.52 and the incident medium is air with n_0 = 1.00. The layer thicknesses d_i and the vacuum wavelength λ are measured in nanometres.</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:ind w:firstLine="200"/><w:jc w:val="both"/><w:spacing w:after="120"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:sz w:val="19"/><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">The optical phase thickness of layer i is δ_i = 2π n_i d_i / λ. With the optical admittance at normal incidence η_i = n_i, the characteristic matrix of the layer is M_i = [[cos δ_i, i sin δ_i / η_i], [i η_i sin δ_i, cos δ_i]]. The multilayer matrix is the ordered product M = M_1 M_2 M_3 M_4 (light travels from layer 1 towards the substrate), the input admittance is Y = (M_21 + M_22 η_s)/(M_11 + M_12 η_s), and the amplitude reflection coefficient and reflectance are r = (η_0 - Y)/(η_0 + Y) and R = |r|². The implementation (src/tmm.py) evaluates the product layer by layer for whole batches of designs and wavelengths at once. Six unit checks are included in tests/test_tmm.py: a zero-thickness stack reproduces the bare air/glass Fresnel reflectance ((n_0-n_s)/(n_0+n_s))² = 0.0426, a single quarter-wave layer reproduces the analytic value R = ((η_0 - n²/n_s)/(η_0 + n²/n_s))², batched and single-design evaluation agree to 1e-13, and 400 random stacks satisfy 0 &lt;= R &lt;= 1 at all 41 wavelengths. Because the model is lossless, R is used directly as the quantity to be learned.</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:ind w:firstLine="0"/><w:jc w:val="center"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:noProof/><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:drawing><wp:inline distT="0" distB="0" distL="0" distR="0"><wp:extent cx="6316220" cy="2098401"/><wp:effectExtent l="0" t="0" r="0" b="0"/><wp:docPr id="2" name="Picture 2"/><wp:cNvGraphicFramePr><a:graphicFrameLocks xmlns:a="http://schemas.openxmlformats.org/drawingml/2006/main" noChangeAspect="1"/></wp:cNvGraphicFramePr><a:graphic xmlns:a="http://schemas.openxmlformats.org/drawingml/2006/main"><a:graphicData uri="http://schemas.openxmlformats.org/drawingml/2006/picture"><pic:pic xmlns:pic="http://schemas.openxmlformats.org/drawingml/2006/picture"><pic:nvPicPr><pic:cNvPr id="2" name="fig2_model_and_data.png"/><pic:cNvPicPr/></pic:nvPicPr><pic:blipFill><a:blip r:embed="rIdFig2"/><a:stretch><a:fillRect/></a:stretch></pic:blipFill><pic:spPr><a:xfrm><a:off x="0" y="0"/><a:ext cx="6316220" cy="2098401"/></a:xfrm><a:prstGeom prst="rect"><a:avLst/></a:prstGeom></pic:spPr></pic:pic></a:graphicData></a:graphic></wp:inline></w:drawing></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:ind w:firstLine="0"/><w:jc w:val="center"/><w:spacing w:before="60" w:after="180"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:b/><w:sz w:val="18"/><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">Figure 2. This split is used for every experiment in the paper and is never re-shuffled.</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:pStyle w:val="21"/><w:ind w:firstLine="0"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">2.4 MLP surrogate model</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:ind w:firstLine="200"/><w:jc w:val="both"/><w:spacing w:after="120"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:sz w:val="19"/><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">The surrogate is a fully connected network with input dimension 4 (the four layer thicknesses, standardised to [-1, 1] with the fixed constants 110 nm and 70 nm, so that the input scaling does not depend on the training subset), hidden layers 128, 128 and 64 with ReLU activations, and 41 output units, i.e. the architecture 4-128-128-64-41 recommended by the assignment; it has 28,073 trainable parameters. A representative case with relatively large MLP prediction error (rank 1 of the 500 test samples, per-sample MSE 5.33e-04).</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:ind w:firstLine="200"/><w:jc w:val="both"/><w:spacing w:after="120"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:sz w:val="19"/><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">The worst of the 500 test samples is shown in Figure 8. Its per-sample MSE is 5.33e-04 (RMSE 0.0231) with a single-wavelength deviation of up to 0.0619 - about 3 times the average test RMSE - for the thicknesses [103.2, 179.8, 178.0, 142.4] nm. Two features of the failure are visible in Figure 8B. First, the error changes sign from one fringe to the next instead of growing with the reflectance itself, which is the signature of a small error in the *position* of the interference fringes rather than of a wrong overall level: the predicted curve is slightly stretched, so it overshoots at some wavelengths and undershoots at neighbouring ones. A shift of the fringe pattern is exactly what a slight mis-estimate of the optical thickness n_i d_i produces, and the four-dimensional input makes this the dominant error mode of the surrogate. Second, the failure is concentrated in the short-wavelength half of the spectrum, where the fringes are sharpest: within the 10 nm sampling interval the reflectance can change by more than 0.05, so the network has to place the extremum to better than one sample. Figure 8C places the sample in the error distribution: the median per-sample MSE is 6.80e-05 and the 95th percentile 1.68e-04, so the worst case is roughly 8 times the median but still within one order of magnitude of it, i.e. the surrogate degrades gracefully rather than failing catastrophically. A denser wavelength sampling would remove part of this error.</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:pStyle w:val="1"/><w:ind w:firstLine="0"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">4. Discussion</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:ind w:firstLine="200"/><w:jc w:val="both"/><w:spacing w:after="120"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:sz w:val="19"/><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">Regarding the first research question, the surrogate reproduces the TMM spectra of unseen designs with a test RMSE of 0.0089, about 3.0 % of the mean reflectance and only 1.4 % of the largest reflectance in the dataset. Figure 5 shows that the residual error is dominated by the placement and the sharpness of the interference fringes rather than by a wrong overall level: the error changes sign from fringe to fringe (section 3.4), which is what a small mis-estimate of the optical thickness n_i d_i produces. Because the mapping thickness -&gt; spectrum is many-to-one in the opposite direction (different stacks can give very similar spectra), part of this residual is irreducible for any deterministic regressor, and an ensemble or a probabilistic output would be needed to go clearly below it.</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:ind w:firstLine="200"/><w:jc w:val="both"/><w:spacing w:after="120"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:sz w:val="19"/><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">Regarding the second question, more data help, but with strongly diminishing returns (Figure 6): the error shrinks quickly between 500 and 1,000 samples and then flattens, with the last doubling of the training set buying only 41 % of MSE. This is the expected behaviour of a smooth function approximator: with 500 samples the network cannot cover the four-dimensional thickness cube densely enough, whereas beyond a few thousand samples the error becomes dominated by the architecture and by the optimisation rather than by the data. It also means that for the present problem a practitioner should first improve the model or the input representation (for instance by adding physically motivated features such as the optical thickness n_i d_i) before generating tens of thousands of additional TMM spectra.</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:ind w:firstLine="200"/><w:jc w:val="both"/><w:spacing w:after="120"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:sz w:val="19"/><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">Regarding the third question, the surrogate is clearly useful for screening. Ranking 10,000 candidates is x13.7 cheaper with the MLP than with TMM, and the design that survives TMM verification reaches 0.6566 - above the 99.9th percentile of the pool. The surrogate therefore does the part of the work where speed matters (pruning a huge library down to a handful of finalists) and leaves the part where accuracy matters to the physical model. Figure 7B is the quantitative justification: the MLP ranks the ten finalists almost, but not exactly, as TMM does - its own favourite is demoted to second place - and its predicted R_target of the best design differs from the verified value by 0.0069. A design reported on the basis of the surrogate alone would therefore be optimistic and, worse, could name the wrong winner among nearly equivalent candidates; the final numbers in Table 1 must come from TMM. This split of responsibilities - surrogate for search, physics for verification - is the same pattern that is used in the deep-learning-assisted inverse-design literature [4-7].</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:ind w:firstLine="200"/><w:jc w:val="both"/><w:spacing w:after="120"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:sz w:val="19"/><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">The study has clear limitations. The optical model is deliberately minimal: no dispersion, no absorption, normal incidence only, and the refractive indices and the number of layers are fixed, so the surrogate learns a four-dimensional map and nothing else. The dataset of 5,000 TMM spectra covers the thickness cube uniformly, but real design problems concentrate on a small region of that cube, where the local data density - and hence the accuracy - would be lower. The failure case in Figure 8 shows the consequence: the surrogate is least reliable exactly for the sharp spectral features that are most interesting for filters. Finally, the MLP was trained with a single seed and a single architecture, so the reported error bars reflect the split rather than the variability of the training procedure; repetitions with different initialisations would be needed to separate the two.</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:pStyle w:val="1"/><w:ind w:firstLine="0"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">5. Conclusions</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:ind w:firstLine="200"/><w:jc w:val="both"/><w:spacing w:after="120"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:sz w:val="19"/><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">A four-layer Air/H/L/H/L/Glass dielectric stack with fixed refractive indices was used to test whether a small MLP can act as a surrogate for the transfer-matrix method. (Q1) Yes: the 4-128-128-64-41 network with 28,073 parameters predicts the 41-point reflectance spectrum of unseen designs with a test RMSE of 0.0089 in reflectance units (3.0 % of the mean reflectance), with a mean absolute error of only 0.0078 at the personal target wavelength 480 nm. (Q2) More training data help but with sharply diminishing returns: the test RMSE drops from 0.0289 at 500 samples to 0.0089 at 4,000 samples, while the last doubling reduces the MSE by only 41 %. (Q3) Yes, with verification: screening 10,000 candidates with the surrogate is x13.7 faster than with TMM, and the selected design reaches a TMM-verified reflectance of 0.6566 at 480 nm, above the 99.9th percentile of the candidate pool, although the final ranking must always be recomputed with the physical model.</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:pStyle w:val="1"/><w:ind w:firstLine="0"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">Data and Code Availability</w:t></w:r></w:p><w:p><w:pPr><w:ind w:firstLine="200"/><w:jc w:val="both"/><w:spacing w:after="120"/></w:pPr><w:r><w:rPr><w:sz w:val="19"/><w:rFonts w:ascii="Calibri" w:hAnsi="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">The data used in this work were generated using the TMM code developed in this study.
